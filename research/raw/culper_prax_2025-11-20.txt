Introduction to Praxis, ulixacaltamide, and Essential Tremor 
 
Praxis was founded in 2015 as EpiPM Therapeutics, and now refers to itself as “a clinical-stage biopharmaceutical 
company translating genetic insights into the development of therapies for central nervous system (CNS) disorders 
characterized by neuronal excitation-inhibition imbalance.” 
 
The Company’s flagship drug candidate is ulixacaltamide (“ulixa”; “PRAX-944”). In January 2018, Praxis acquired 
ulix (“PRAX-944”) from EPIRUS Biopharmaceuticals, Inc (EPRS.Q) – which had spent several years studying the 
compound for potential use in neuropathic pain – for a mere $1 million. We find the paltry price tag indicative  of 
its poor clinical prospects. 
 
In October 2020, Praxis completed a $190 million IPO on the NASDAQ GS, touting PRAX-944 as one of its leading 
drug candidates for potential use in treating essential tremor (“ET”).  ET is a nervous system disorder affecting 
roughly 7 million people in the U.S., primarily those above 65 years of age. Its primary symptoms are trembling in 
the hands, such that the disorder is often confused with Parkinson’s. ET is thought to be caused by a gene alteration 
passed from parent to child. Current first line medications include beta blockers such as propranolol and anti-seizure 
medications such as primidone and gabapentin.  According to Praxis , up to 50% of ET patients don’t receive 
treatment altogether and 77% of ET patients don’t feel their symptoms are manageable with current treatments. 
 
Ulixa is a T-type calcium channel inhibitor, which the Company argues is linked to the abnormal brain activity that 
manifests with ET. While we acknowledge t hat neurology is incredibly complex, we have seen little evidence to 
suggest that this mech anism can succeed  in ET. On the contrary, three  drugs with the same mechanism have 
already been tested and failed earlier stage clinical trials in ET. 
 
- Jazz Pharmaceuticals plc (JAZZ) studied  suvecaltamide (JZP385), a highly selective and state-dependent 
modulator of T-type calcium channels, in adult patients with ET. Suvecaltamide did not achieve statistical 
significance at 30mg versus placebo on the primary endpoint or the key secondary endpoint. One participant 
experienced a serious adverse event considered treatment related by the investigator. 
 
- Neurocrine Biosciences studied apinocaltamide (NBI-827104), a triple -T calcium channel blocker. The 
drug failed to meet primary or secondary efficacy endpoints in Neurocrine’s Phase 2 study.   
 
- Ethosuximide, another T-type calcium channel inhibitor, failed to show that it was an effective treatment. 
An open label trial initially planned to enroll 15 patients, but stopped the trial after 7 patients participated 
due to “lack of efficacy and a high incidence of adverse effects”.     
 
Tellingly, Praxis’ earlier trials of ulixa have shown mixed results, at best. In March 2023, Praxis reported negative 
top-line results from a Phase 2b dose -range-finding trial of ulixa for ET (“Essential1”). The study measured the 
change in TETRAS modified Activities of Daily Living (“mADL”) in 132 patients over 56 days.  Ulixacaltamide 
failed to produce a statistically significant difference to placebo (p=0.126). Shares fell over 40% overnight. 1

Yet Praxis employed a mixed model for repeated measures (“MMRM”)  under a missing -at-random assumption 
(“MAR”). This imputation method, per the Company, treats the “subject as a random effect with an unstructured 
covariance matrix.”  In layman’s terms, the model fills missing values with data that assumes patients who 
dropped from the study would have performed similarly to those who remained.4 In our view, this is a wildly 
aggressive, unsuitable methodology that artificially inflates results. 
 
Numerous former FDA officials we spoke with suggested that this methodology was wholly unsuitable, especially 
given the massive disparity in dropout rates between the groups. 5 Per one former 10+ year FDA official, 
 
“When I read the Company’ s claim, they claim they use MMRM, and that analysis will definitely not be 
accepted by the FDA.  The definition is random –  it assumes both treatment and placebo drop out  [at 
equivalent rates]. And if you claim you’re getting a positive result with that methodology, they [Praxis] 
won’t get the FDA [approval]. Because it’ s 35% [treatment dropout] vs. 5% [placebo dropout], it’ s clearly 
due to the adverse events in the treatment group. It’s clearly not correct to use that… You need to be missing 
randomly. Missingness is much higher in treatment group than placebo, so the high dropout is not missing 
randomly.”
