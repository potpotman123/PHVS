## 6. Perform a skeptical clinical evidence audit

Build a compact evidence table for the decision-relevant trials and extensions.

Capture:

- Trial identifier and phase.
- Formulation, dose, and regimen.
- Randomized and analyzed populations.
- Eligibility criteria and baseline characteristics.
- Comparator and background treatment.
- Follow-up and exposure.
- Primary endpoint and estimand: precisely what treatment effect the analysis aims to estimate.
- Prespecified secondary endpoints and multiplicity control.
- Effect sizes and uncertainty.
- Rescue medication rules.
- Missing-data methods.
- Discontinuations and reasons.
- Safety findings.
- Protocol or analysis changes.
- Source and disclosure date.

Then investigate the following.

### Population and denominator integrity

Reconcile patients screened, enrolled, randomized, dosed, evaluable, completing treatment, and included in each analysis.

Identify whether each headline result uses intention-to-treat, modified intention-to-treat, per-protocol, completer, observed-case, or another population.

For attack-based trials, distinguish numbers of patients from numbers of attacks. Repeated attacks in the same patient are not independent observations.

Ask whether the headline denominator differs from the denominator a reader would naturally assume.

### Dropouts, missingness, and rescue treatment

Determine whether discontinuations differ by arm, timing, baseline severity, or reason.

Could intolerance or nonresponse selectively remove patients? Could rescue treatment or censoring make the observed outcome appear more favorable?

Evaluate the actual missing-data assumptions. Where appropriate, propose tipping-point analyses, plausible bounds, or alternative assumptions.

Do not automatically count every missing patient as a failure or assume missingness is benign. Explain which assumptions fit the clinical setting.

Identify whether extension-study results are affected by survivor selection, treatment crossover, or loss of a concurrent comparator.

### Endpoint selection and changes

Compare registry history, protocol, statistical analysis plan, publications, and investor materials where available.

Distinguish:

- Prospectively specified changes made before relevant data access.
- Changes made during a blinded study.
- Changes made after unblinding.
- Post-hoc analyses presented as confirmatory.

A registry update alone does not establish when a decision was made or who had access to data. Report uncertainty rather than alleging misconduct without evidence.

Ask whether the emphasized endpoint is the primary endpoint, whether the time window changed, and whether omitted outcomes would alter interpretation.

### Effect size and robustness

Report absolute and relative effects where appropriate.

For prophylaxis, examine attack rates, baseline burden, attack-free proportions, severe attacks, rescue use, and durability.

For on-demand treatment, examine time to relief, meaningful relief, complete resolution, redosing, rescue use, recurrence, and consistency across attacks.

Distinguish statistical success from clinical importance and commercial differentiation.

Use methods suited to the endpoint: binary response, recurrent-event counts, time-to-event outcomes, or longitudinal measurements. Examine overdispersion, clustering, baseline adjustment, and censoring when relevant.

Do not run a simple test on reconstructed counts and claim it reproduces a trial’s prespecified adjusted analysis. Label it as an approximation and state the differences.

### Power and replication

For future studies, distinguish planned power from predictive probability of success.

Account for uncertainty in effect size, placebo response, patient mix, variance, and multiplicity. Consider regression to the mean and winner’s curse after small favorable studies.

Do not use post-hoc “observed power” as an independent interpretation of completed trial results.

## 7. Investigate cohorts without manufacturing subgroup discoveries

Look for clinically justified heterogeneity in both benefit and commercial relevance.

Potential dimensions include HAE subtype, baseline attack frequency, prior prophylaxis, prior treatment response, attack severity and location, treatment timing, age, geography, adherence, exposure, and patient preference.

For each proposed cohort analysis:

1. Explain the biological or behavioral rationale.
2. State whether it was prespecified.
3. Identify actual subgroup data available.
4. Report sample sizes and uncertainty.
5. Distinguish a prognostic factor from a predictor of differential treatment benefit.
6. Seek a treatment-by-subgroup interaction rather than comparing “significant here” with “not significant there.”
7. Examine whether the result survives reasonable cutoff changes.
8. Identify independent replication or prospective validation.
9. Explain whether the cohort can be identified in routine practice.
10. Explain its consequence for label, access, uptake, persistence, or revenue.

Consider Simpson’s paradox, baseline imbalance, geographic mix, and whether a result is driven by a few high-frequency patients.

Keep three categories separate:

- Observed subgroup evidence.
- Hypotheses consistent with aggregate evidence.
- Hypothetical scenarios created to test sensitivity.

If individual-patient data are unavailable, say which analyses cannot be performed. Do not invent patient records or present synthetic data as reconstructed clinical evidence.

For mixture calculations, explicitly show the identification problem. An equation such as:

Overall response = subgroup share × subgroup response + other-patient share × other-patient response

does not uniquely identify its unknown inputs. Explore plausible ranges and competing explanations rather than selecting the combination that supports a preferred thesis.

## 11. Model the interaction between prophylaxis and on-demand treatment

This is a central opportunity for deeper analysis.

A patient may use both prophylaxis and rescue therapy. Counting that patient in both treatment categories can be appropriate, but their attack rate and utilization must remain internally consistent.

Investigate:

- Whether improved prophylaxis reduces the total number of treated attacks.
- Whether oral on-demand treatment increases the proportion of attacks patients treat.
- Whether earlier treatment changes redosing, rescue use, or recurrence.
- Whether some patients might defer prophylaxis when on-demand treatment becomes less burdensome.
- Whether a shared franchise improves access or prescribing familiarity.
- Whether combined use creates distinct safety, labeling, or payer considerations.
- Whether initial home inventory and replenishment differ materially from actual consumption.

Develop coherent scenarios in which one formulation succeeds while reducing part of the opportunity for the other.

Do not independently maximize prophylaxis penetration, residual attack frequency, on-demand treatment share, and price if those assumptions conflict.

### Clinical evidence to commercial inputs

Explain how an eventual SOTP should avoid double counting shared costs, overlapping indications, and patient populations.

Use an explicit scenario framework such as:

Expected operating value = sum across coherent scenarios of scenario probability × discounted scenario cash flows.

Explain that scenario probabilities must sum to one and that costs are incurred along their actual development paths. A failed program can still consume substantial cash before failure.

Distinguish explicit failure probabilities from the discount rate. Avoid applying multiple arbitrary haircuts to the same risk.

Explain the eventual bridge from operating value to equity value, including cash, debt, commitments, future funding requirements, and dilution.

## My background and how to explain the analysis

I have some background in molecular and cellular biology and physiology, but I am not fluent at the level of an MD or PhD. Build on that foundation without assuming specialist knowledge of immunology, pharmacology, clinical medicine, or biostatistics.

Preserve the scientific rigor and depth. Make the reasoning accessible through explanation rather than omitting difficult concepts.

- Introduce disease-specific biology before using it to support an investment argument. Explain what the relevant cells, proteins, receptors, and pathways do, and why those functions matter here.
- Define specialist terms and acronyms on first use. A definition alone is often insufficient: explain the concept’s role in the argument.
- Walk through the causal links between molecular action, physiological effect, clinical outcome, and commercial consequence. Identify where a link is established and where it remains a hypothesis.
- For statistical methods, first explain the question the method answers and why the obvious simpler comparison might mislead. Then provide the technical interpretation, assumptions, and limitations.
- For equations, define variables and units and work through a small example when helpful. Distinguish what the calculation demonstrates from what it cannot establish.
- When interpreting a trial result or challenging a claim, show the evidence and inferential steps clearly enough that I can assess the argument myself.
- Use analogies selectively, and explain where they break down. Do not let an intuitive analogy substitute for biological evidence.

Keep explanations close to the analysis they support. Avoid a long introductory textbook chapter or excessive explanations of elementary biology. The goal is for me to understand both the conclusion and how an informed analyst reaches—or rejects—it.

The objective is to identify the small number of clinical and commercial facts that could change what this company is worth—and determine how confidently we can know them.
