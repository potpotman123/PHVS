“I can see how this one asset and the financing would be questionable, but there’s no way any of those guys would work there or be on the board if they didn’t have something amazeballs going on.“ - Person I am not attacking personally; this just so happens to be the most recent instance I’ve read of an all-too-prevalent line of thinking.

The Nature of This Business
It is well known that data analysis is principally important in biotech analysis insofar as the data is quantitative clinical data from the trial(s) of the company of interest, or any potentially relevant quantitative data from other scientific publications. The underappreciated but also incredibly important kind of data analysis is that which pertains to the interpretation and subsequent weighing of all relevant qualitative data, and this is where the unseasoned and the fatuous regularly expose themselves in discourse.

Before I get into this, an understanding of the nature of this business must be established. There are three axioms that must be ingrained in your mind to prevent yourself from falling into common reasoning traps. Each is argued for once below, so that from then on it can be held as an axiom.

The first concerns the company’s incentives. A biotech company has an enormous financial stake in what investors & partners believe about its drug. Its ability to raise capital, sign deals, retain talent, and survive long enough to reach its next readout depends on that collective belief. Transparency is always in your best interest as the investor. It is not necessarily in the company’s , and often it is the case that it is not. Thus, the actions a company takes and the information it communicates, including the way it presents its quantitative data, are frequently the best representation of the company’s interests rather than the best representation of the truth. It rarely has to lie to accomplish this. A relative risk reduction can stand in for an unimpressive absolute one, a per-protocol analysis for a disappointing intent-to-treat result, a post-hoc subgroup for a population that failed to produce a stat-sig p-value on primary endpoints. Selection, emphasis, and framing rarely cross a legal line, and they are usually enough to sway many in those aforementioned parties.

Axiom 1: These companies are not your friends, and nothing they say can be taken at face value or without several large grains of salt.

The second concerns base rates. More than nine in ten drugs that enter clinical trials never reach approval. Nearly every one of those failures was designed, run, and championed by doctors and scientists, many of them acclaimed, and many were endorsed by the most respected key opinion leaders (KOLs) in their fields. The appeal to authority, in whatever form it takes, rests on the premise that a drug backed by accomplished, credentialed people will probably work. This statistic alone falsifies that premise. Credentialed backing is present in nearly every program, winners and losers alike, so it cannot be what separates one from the other.

History also tells us that an entire field can be wrong at once. Low HDL cholesterol was one of the most consistent predictors of heart disease in epidemiology. For well over a decade, four of the largest drug companies in the world pursued the hypothesis that raising it would prevent heart attacks. Pfizer’s torcetrapid increased mortality, Roche’s dalcetrapib & Lilly’s evacetrapib were stopped for futility despite raising HDL substantially. Merck’s anacetrapib produced modest benefit consistent with its LDL lowering rather than its HDL raising, they never filed it.

Along the way, genetic evidence suggested HDL had never been causal at all. Earlier still, in the 1980s, suppressing the premature heartbeats that predicted sudden death after a heart attack seemed so obviously beneficial that drugs doing it were widely prescribed. Then the CAST trial found that patients taking them were more likely to die.

More recently, Novo Nordisk’s ZEUS trial failed to prevent MACE with a hazard ratio of 0.99, shocking the field, despite its profound ability to decrease hsCRP, a highly predictive biomarker that had never had such a biomarker/patient outcome correlation break in a trial that large theretofore. Axiom 2: No matter who is running the company or what the KOLs say, they are fallible, and yes, they can, in fact, all be wrong.

The third concerns management’s own incentives, which are not necessarily the same as the company’s, let alone yours. Management gets paid whether the drug works or not. Yes, their options may expire worthless, but the salary they collect in the interim is, for many, incentive enough to keep the show going for as long as possible: one more trial run in a sub-group that produced a p value <0.05, one more financing, one more pivot to a new indication. A company with a failed drug and cash in the bank would often serve its shareholders best by returning that cash, and that is rarely management’s first instinct. None of this requires dishonesty. If twenty hours of work can bias an analyst toward his own conclusions (see: Biotech Analysis, Part 1), consider what a decade of work, a professional identity, and a salary can do to the people running the program.

Axiom 3: Management gets paid as long as the company survives, whether or not the drug works, so never mistake a program’s persistence for its promise.

Now that these three axioms have been stated clearly, we can proceed to the main topic of discussion.

In part 1, I described what biotech analysis actually consists of. Knowledge is vocabulary; the edge lies in what you do with it. You interrogate arguments, analyze their premises, and determine whether the question being asked is even valid before you try to answer it. You identify hidden assumptions, test alternative explanations, and determine what the evidence actually supports. You ask what a biomarker represents and whether its relationship with disease is causal or merely correlative. You treat the literature as fallible and scrutinize it as hard as the asset itself. Above all, you attack your own conclusions with extreme prejudice, because no one else is going to do it for you.

Notice what this process does not contain. At no step does it ask who is making an argument. It evaluates premises, evidence, and inferences, and it has no step for credentials, because credentials are not evidence about the drug. An argument from a Nobel laureate and the same argument from an undergraduate are the same argument, and they succeed or fail on the same terms.

This matters, because in this industry the people making the arguments are unusually impressive. Biotech companies are routinely run by accredited MDs and PhDs, former heads of major health agencies and research institutions, and veterans of the largest pharmaceutical companies in the world, and they are advised by the most decorated KOLs in their fields. This leaves the many who are not privy to how this business works highly susceptible to an incredibly dangerous form of appeal to authority: the belief that the credentials of the people behind a drug are evidence that the drug works.

To be precise about what I am attacking: deference to expertise is not always a fallacy. In part 1, I argued that when your analysis conflicts with the literature, the literature is more likely right, because even flawed literature has survived external review while a fresh conclusion has survived none. That reasoning generalizes: weight a source by the scrutiny it has survived and the independence of its incentives. The dangerous form of the appeal to authority violates exactly this. It cites the credentials of interested parties, whose statements have survived no external review at all, as though they could settle a question at the frontier of science (in case you forgot where it is we operate). That kind of deference feels like humility. It is not humility; it is abdication, and it must be eradicated from your thinking entirely.

Its most common forms will be familiar to anyone who has spent time in this industry.

“You think the doctors and scientists are wrong and you’re right?” This question smuggles in two premises. The first is that a company’s public statements are the sincere, fully informed, and unbiased beliefs of its scientists, which Axioms 1 and 3 should have disabused you of. The second is that “the doctors” agree with one another. They do not. The scientists at the competitor, the FDA reviewers, and the academic skeptics are doctors too. The analyst is not pitting himself against medicine; he is choosing between doctors, on the basis of arguments. And per Axiom 2, nearly every drug that has ever failed was backed by a room full of doctors and scientists who said it would work.

“You think hundreds of millions would be allocated to Dr. So-and-so if the drug wasn’t going to work?” Yes, and it happens constantly. Funding is not a forecast that a drug will work; it is a bet on expected value at a price. An investor can rationally back a program with a one-in-four chance of success if the payoff is large enough, and stand by that decision when it fails, as most such bets do. Investors can even make money on drugs that ultimately fail, by selling into a strong Phase 2 readout, an IPO, or a partnership before Phase 3 delivers its verdict. And the more than nine in ten drugs that never reach approval were funded too.

“I called management, and they said…” Reread Axioms 1 and 3. What management tells you costs them nothing to say, is said by people with every incentive to say it, and would be said whether or not the drug works, so it cannot tell you whether the drug works. Moreover, Regulation FD prohibits public companies from selectively disclosing material nonpublic information to investors and analysts. Whatever you heard on that call was either already public, immaterial, or a compliance violation, and if it was the last, that tells you something about management in its own right.

None of these contains a single fact about the drug. Each is a claim about people: their credentials, their capital, their words. That brings us to the question of weight.

Assigning Weight
Every analysis of a developmental-stage asset requires weighing several kinds of evidence, and it is worth being explicit about how much each deserves.

The quantitative clinical data is what the drug actually did in actual patients. Together with the relevant scientific literature (determining what other literature is relevant is no trivial task, and often times the answer is not obvious [for instance, perhaps looking at AIDS patients with comorbidity x could be informative on how a CD4 inhibitor would perform in said disease x], and there is not necessarily an objective right or wrong answer; it would be quite impossible to truly analyze all relevant literature, as the literature is too vast), it is the closest thing you have to direct evidence of whether the drug works, and it is read with every tool from part 1: endpoints interrogated, biomarkers questioned for causality, results set against base rates. It carries the most weight because it is produced by biology rather than by people talking about biology.

The quality of management matters, but for a narrower question than most people assume. Good management designs trials capable of detecting real efficacy, chooses endpoints wisely, enrolls efficiently, navigates regulators, finances the company without destroying it, and launches competently. All of this bears on whether a working drug succeeds. None of it bears on whether the drug works. Brilliant management can give a good molecule its best chance; it cannot make an ineffective molecule effective. And management quality is judged by track record, not résumé: how well these people have executed before, and how honestly they have reported their results.

Titles carry the least weight of all. A title is weak evidence of competence and no evidence whatsoever of candor or independence, which, per Axioms 1 and 3, are precisely the qualities in question whenever management speaks. Nor does a title have any bearing on the biology. The molecule doesn’t read the CEO’s CV.

So when you hear “they are brilliant doctors with decades of experience, there is no way this drug fails, and you are not a doctor, so you cannot be right,” recognize it as a fallacy: it offers credentials where an argument should be. And yet in many minds it remains the preferable position, and not without reason. It is genuinely hard to believe that one person, a person with no impressive title, can look at a room full of accomplished authority figures, tell them they are wrong, and be anything other than arrogant. In most of life, deference to experts is the rational policy, and most people who believe they have out-thought a room full of experts have not. So the real question is not whether you may ever disagree with the room, but when, and on what grounds. Answering it requires returning to a question I raised in part 1.

In part 1, I asked: “What makes an argument valid? What makes an argument sound? How can an argument be valid without being sound?” An argument is valid when its conclusion follows necessarily from its premises, so that the premises cannot all be true while the conclusion is false. It is sound when it is valid and its premises are in fact true, in which case its conclusion must be true. An argument is valid without being sound when its form is flawless but a premise is false. Recall the HDL story from Axiom 2: “Anything that raises HDL reduces cardiovascular risk; CETP inhibitors raise HDL; therefore, CETP inhibitors reduce cardiovascular risk.” The argument is perfectly valid. An entire field and four of the largest drug companies in the world built programs on it. Its first premise was false.

Strictly speaking, though, the conclusions that matter in biotech analysis cannot be sound. Soundness belongs to deductive arguments, whose premises guarantee their conclusions. Ours do not. Our conclusions are probabilistic (this drug will probably fail; this trial is more likely than not to succeed), so the reasoning behind them is inductive, and even true premises cannot guarantee an inductive conclusion.

For the remainder of this article, then, I will use “sound” to mean “as sound as can be,” which I define as comparatively more plausible than every other argument interrogated, and in particular, than the arguments presented by the company and its KOLs. Correspondingly, an argument “breaks” yours when it renders yours no longer the most plausible one on the table.

With that definition in hand: if, and only if, you are fit to be a biotech analyst, then once you have determined that your argument is superior to management’s, and especially if you are confident they are flat wrong, you can dismiss their words as noise. Not because you are smarter than they are, but because understanding the qualitative data means arriving at an inevitable conclusion: its weight relative to the quantitative data, and to the arguments that best flow from the quantitative data and the literature you deem relevant, is dismal. That stands in stark contrast to what the grandiosity of the titles, the confidence of the executives, and the size of the checks would lead you to expect at first glance.

Read that “if, and only if” twice, because the entire argument rests on it. Whether you are fit to be a biotech analyst is not a question you can settle by introspection. It can only be settled definitively by track record. Conviction is not evidence of competence; nearly everyone who has been badly wrong felt right at the time. A track record is evidence: a long history of calls, made in advance and checked against outcomes, showing whether the things you judged 70% likely actually happened about 70% of the time. This is the same standard I asked you to apply to management. You judge them by what they have done rather than by their titles, and you must judge yourself by your record rather than by your conviction. Until you have that record, hold your conclusions accordingly.

Human judgment is imperfect, and yours is no exception. You can always be wrong, no matter how thorough your work, how sound your argument, or how long your record. But despite this, if and only if you are truly fit to be a biotech analyst, the reasoning is superior to the alternative (weighing management’s apparent judgment over your own), and it rests on a simple syllogism:

Premise 1: If I am a competent analyst, and I have done sufficient research to arrive at a most probable conclusion, then my conclusion holds weight over all others, unless I am presented with an argument that breaks mine.

Premise 2: I am a competent analyst, and I have done sufficient research to arrive at a most probable conclusion.

Conclusion: Therefore, my conclusion holds weight over all others, unless I am presented with an argument that breaks mine.

The form is valid; it is modus ponens (if P, then Q; P, therefore Q). Whether it is sound, in the sense defined above, depends on its premises, and above all on the second, which is exactly why the track record matters: it is the only way to establish that premise rather than merely assert it. Nor is this as arrogant as it sounds. Your conclusion holds weight over management’s not because your opinion outranks theirs, but because your analysis is where theirs was weighed. And the exception clause is not a formality. When you are presented with an argument that breaks yours, your argument is broken, no matter whose mouth it came from. A title cannot break your argument, but an argument can, whoever holds the title, and you must be as ready (and enthusiastic, because you’re in this to make money, not to ego trip… right?) to concede to a good argument from management as you are to dismiss an appeal to their titles.

With all of this established, we arrive at the point. The underappreciated but also incredibly important kind of data analysis is that which pertains to the interpretation and subsequent weighing of all relevant qualitative data. The titles and pedigrees, the capital raised, the conviction on the conference call, management’s reassurances, the KOLs’ endorsements: all of it is qualitative data, and all of it must be interpreted and weighed. The whole point of being good at this kind of analysis is to understand its relative unimportance compared to quantitative data analysis. It is difficult to overstate how important it is that you understand how unimportant it is, relative to the actual science.

The drug will do what it does, no matter who is speaking for it.
